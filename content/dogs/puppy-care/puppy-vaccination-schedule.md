# Puppy Vaccination Schedule: Which Shots, When, and Why the Series Works This Way

Puppies receive their core vaccinations as a **series every 2–4 weeks from about 6–8 weeks of age until at least 16 weeks**: not because one shot is weak, but because maternal antibodies from mom's milk interfere with vaccines on an unpredictable, fading timeline. The series exists to close that gap. Core shots (per AAHA guidelines) protect against distemper, adenovirus, parvovirus, leptospirosis, and rabies (required by law nearly everywhere in the U.S.). Here's the schedule, what each vaccine covers, and the socialization balancing act while immunity builds.

## The schedule at a glance

Core vaccinations run as a series every 2–4 weeks from about 6–8 weeks of age until at least 16 weeks, because maternal antibodies interfere with vaccines on an unpredictable, fading timeline.

| Age | Typical vaccines |
|---|---|
| 6–8 weeks | DAP/DHPP #1 (distemper, adenovirus, parvovirus, ± parainfluenza): often given breeder-side; check your paperwork |
| 10–12 weeks | DAP #2 · non-core by lifestyle: Bordetella, canine influenza, Lyme (regional) |
| 12 weeks | Leptospirosis #1 (core) |
| 14–16 weeks | DAP #3 (**the ≥16-week dose is the one that must not be missed**) · leptospirosis #2 (2–4 weeks after #1) · rabies (state law typically 12–16 weeks) · non-core boosters |
| 12–16 months | DAP booster + rabies booster + leptospirosis booster |
| Adult | Rabies per state law (1–3 yr) · DAP typically every 3 years · leptospirosis annual booster · non-core annually as lifestyle warrants |

Your vet will tailor timing, brands, and the non-core lineup to your region and your puppy's life: this table is the map, not the prescription.

## What the core shots actually prevent

Core shots protect against distemper, adenovirus, parvovirus, leptospirosis, and rabies, which is required by law nearly everywhere in the U.S.

- **Parvovirus**, the reason the series is sacred: a hardy, environment-surviving virus causing severe vomiting and bloody diarrhea, often fatal without treatment, but 70–90% of dogs survive with prompt, intensive veterinary care. Parvo is why "just one shot" and dog parks don't mix.
- **Distemper**: attacks respiratory, GI, and neurological systems; survivors often carry permanent damage.
- **Adenovirus (infectious hepatitis)**: liver disease; the vaccine's "A" earns its place.
- **Rabies**: invariably fatal and zoonotic, which is why it's the one vaccine the law, not just the vet, requires.

**Leptospirosis is core, not optional:** bacterial, spread via wildlife urine and standing water, zoonotic, and can be fatal (acute kidney and liver failure). AAHA's guidelines were updated in 2024 to recommend it for every dog, not just those with rural or high-exposure lifestyles.

**Non-core, worth the conversation:** Bordetella (kennel cough, required by most daycares, boarders, and [puppy classes](/dogs/training/puppy-training/)), canine influenza, and Lyme in tick country.

## The socialization dilemma, solved sensibly

The [socialization window closes around 12–14 weeks](/dogs/training/puppy-training/), *before* the vaccine series finishes. Hiding the puppy until 16+ weeks trades disease risk for a lifetime of behavior risk, which is why the AVSAB explicitly endorses early, controlled socialization and advises avoiding unsanitized or high-traffic areas: **yes** to puppy classes that verify vaccines, carried outings, friends' homes with known vaccinated dogs; **no** to dog parks, pet-store floors, and anywhere unknown dogs concentrate. As general veterinary practice (not a specific AVSAB timeline), most vets suggest waiting until 1–2 weeks after the final parvo shot before unknown-dog territory. Ground rules and outing ideas in the [8-week schedule](/dogs/puppy-care/8-week-old-puppy-schedule/).

## Side effects and costs

**Normal for a day or so:** sleepiness, mild soreness, reduced appetite. **Not normal, vet immediately:** facial swelling, hives, repeated vomiting, difficulty breathing, or collapse (rare acute reactions typically within minutes to hours, [emergency signs guide](/dogs/health/signs-your-dog-needs-a-vet/)). **Cost planning:** commonly $75–$200 per visit depending on region and what's bundled, with low-cost clinics and shelters offering the series for much less; the full first-year picture lives in the [pet cost guide](/pet-guides/pet-costs/), and [insurance or wellness plans](/pet-guides/pet-insurance/) often cover routine vaccines.

> **Veterinary disclaimer:** This page summarizes AAHA-style guidelines for education. Your veterinarian sets your puppy's actual schedule: regional disease risk, state rabies law, and your puppy's history all change the plan. Missed or unknown vaccine history? Don't guess; vets restart or adjust series safely all the time.

## Frequently asked questions

### When can my puppy go outside and meet other dogs?

Controlled socialization should start *before* the series ends: carried outings, vaccine-checked puppy classes, and known vaccinated dogs are AVSAB-endorsed from the start. Unknown-dog territory (parks, pet-store floors) waits until 1–2 weeks after the final 16-week parvo dose.

### What vaccines are puppies legally required to have?

Rabies: required by law in nearly every U.S. state; timing and booster interval are set by your state, typically starting no earlier than 12 weeks. Check your state's schedule; AAHA defers entirely to local law here. Everything else is medically driven rather than legally mandated, though boarding, daycare, and travel effectively require the core set plus Bordetella.

### What if my puppy misses a vaccine in the series?

Call your vet: depending on the gap they'll continue or restart, and it's routine either way. The critical principle: the final DAP dose must land at 16 weeks or later, because earlier doses may have been neutralized by maternal antibodies.

### Are puppy vaccines safe?

Serious reactions are rare, and the diseases they prevent, parvo above all, are common, severe, and expensive precisely where vaccination rates drop; parvovirus is often fatal without treatment, but 70–90% of dogs survive with prompt, intensive veterinary care. A sleepy afternoon post-shot is normal; facial swelling, vomiting, or breathing trouble is an immediate vet call.
