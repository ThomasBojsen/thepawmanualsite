# Leash Training: Why Dogs Pull, and How to Make Stopping Pay Better

Dogs pull for one reason: **it works.** Every step forward on a tight leash pays the pulling, hundreds of times per walk. Leash training is simply reversing the economy — a tight leash stops the world, a loose leash and your side become where good things happen. No prong collars, no choke chains, no leash pops: aversive tools carry documented fallout (the AVSAB's position), and a front-clip harness plus this method handles even determined pullers. Expect real change in two to four weeks of consistent walks.

## Gear first (it's half the battle)

- **Front-clip harness** — the pulling-management workhorse: redirects forward momentum sideways without throat pressure. Fitting and picks in the [harness guide](/dogs/products/best-dog-harnesses/). Mandatory for flat-faced breeds like [Frenchies](/dogs/breeds/french-bulldog/) — collars and compromised airways don't mix.
- **A standard 4–6 ft leash** — retractable leashes *teach* pulling (tension is their default state) and belong nowhere near training.
- **A collar for tags** stays on regardless.
- **A treat pouch** — you'll reinforce dozens of times per session, and fumbling pockets ruins timing.

## The method, step by step

1. **Charge your side (indoors, day 1–3):** stand still, drop treats at your left heel repeatedly. Walk three steps, treat at heel. You're making a location valuable before asking anything.
2. **Red light, green light (from day 3):** leash tightens → you stop dead, silently. Leash slackens (dog turns, steps back) → instantly mark, treat at your side, walk on. The dog learns tension is a brake pedal it controls.
3. **Direction changes:** before the leash tightens, cheerfully turn the other way — following you becomes the game and checking in with you becomes a habit.
4. **Reinforce generously, then thin out:** early on, treat every few steps of loose leash. As the habit sets, stretch to intermittent — sniff breaks make superb non-food rewards ("free sniff" on cue is currency).
5. **Graduate the environment:** hallway → yard → quiet street → the real world. Each jump up in distraction, jump down in expectations.

**The consistency clause:** every walk trains, or untrains. If Tuesday's rush lets pulling reach the park, Tuesday taught pulling. When there's genuinely no time, drive or play in the yard instead of drilling the bad habit.

## Puppies vs. adult pullers

[Puppies](/dogs/training/puppy-training/): start indoors at 10–12 weeks with a light leash dragging, then held — it's all games before 16 weeks, and you're building the loose-leash default before pulling ever pays once. Adult rescues with years of pulling mileage: same method, longer runway, more management (front-clip harness from day one) — the economy flips just as reliably, it just has more history to outvote.

**The exception that isn't a pulling problem:** a dog that lunges, barks, or explodes at other dogs, bikes, or people on leash is *reactive* — that's fear or frustration, not manners, and red-light-green-light won't touch it. Distance is your friend short-term; a certified positive-reinforcement trainer or veterinary behaviorist is the real answer, especially since [pain can masquerade as reactivity](/dogs/health/signs-your-dog-needs-a-vet/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to leash train a dog?

Two to four weeks of consistent walks for solid loose-leash habits in most dogs — longer for adult dogs with years of practiced pulling. Consistency is the variable: one "just this once" pulling success resets the meter.

### Is a harness better than a collar for leash training?

Yes — a front-clip harness redirects pulling without throat pressure and is non-negotiable for flat-faced breeds. Prong and choke collars are off the table: the AVSAB warns aversive tools risk fear and aggression fallout, and they don't teach the dog where you'd like it to be.

### Why does my dog bite the leash on walks?

Usually arousal or play — the leash is the nearest tug toy. Keep it boring (no tug-back), reinforce heavily for walking with a mouth-free leash, and give the mouth a job: carry-toy walks genuinely work. Persistent frantic leash-biting often signals an over-stimulated dog that needs shorter, calmer outings first.

### My dog is stronger than me — what do I do?

Front-clip harness immediately (management beats strength), train in low-distraction places where winning is possible, and consider a few sessions with a positive-reinforcement trainer. Strength stops mattering once pulling stops paying — that's the whole design.
