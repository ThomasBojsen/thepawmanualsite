# How to Potty Train a Puppy (the Boring, Reliable Way)

Potty training is a logistics problem, not an intelligence test: most puppies are reliably trained in 4–8 weeks on three pillars — **a strict schedule, constant supervision, and a reward within two seconds of success outside.** There is no pillar called punishment: scolding accidents doesn't teach "go outside," it teaches "don't let the human see," which produces a dog that sneaks behind the couch. Here's the system, the age-based schedule, and the fixes when it goes sideways.

## The system, step by step

1. **Pick one potty spot** and go there every time — scent builds the habit.
2. **Out on every trigger:** immediately after waking (including naps), within 15 minutes of eating or drinking, after play sessions, and on the clock in between (chart below).
3. **Go out *with* the puppy,** every time, on leash — you must be there within two seconds of the deed to reward it. Sending them out alone is how you end up rewarding a puppy for coming back in.
4. **Boring until business:** stand still, no play. The moment they finish: marker word, great treat, *then* optional play — so pottying starts the fun instead of ending it.
5. **Supervise indoors like it's your job** — it briefly is. Sniffing circles and sudden wandering are the tell. Can't watch? [Crate](/dogs/training/crate-training/) or playpen: dogs avoid soiling their sleeping space, which is why the crate is potty training's best tool.
6. **Log it for a week** if progress stalls — patterns (always 20 minutes after meals, always during TV time) write the schedule for you.

## How often out? The age chart

| Age | Maximum time between trips |
|---|---|
| 8–10 weeks | ~1 hour (overnight stretches longer — 2–3 hours) |
| 3 months | ~2 hours |
| 4 months | ~3 hours |
| 6 months+ | 4–6 hours, working toward the adult rhythm |

Rule of thumb: months of age + 1 = daytime hours a puppy can reasonably hold it, capped around 6 even for adults. The [8-week schedule](/dogs/puppy-care/8-week-old-puppy-schedule/) shows how trips slot into a full day, and consistent [feeding times](/dogs/food/puppy-food/) make output as predictable as input.

## Accidents: the protocol

- **Caught mid-act:** calm interrupt ("outside!"), scoop up, finish at the spot, reward what finishes outside. No anger — you're redirecting, not busting.
- **Found after the fact:** clean it, say nothing. The puppy cannot connect your frustration to a past event; only to you being scary near pee.
- **Clean with an enzymatic cleaner** — ordinary cleaners (especially ammonia-based) leave scent markers that read as "approved toilet." This one product genuinely matters.

## When potty training "fails"

A trained dog that regresses is a message, not a relapse: **rule out a urinary tract infection first** — sudden accidents, frequent small pees, or straining are [vet-visit signs](/dogs/health/signs-your-dog-needs-a-vet/), especially in females. Then check the usual suspects: schedule got loose, freedom expanded too fast (earn the house room by room), stress or routine change, or adolescent brain fog (normal — tighten up for two weeks). Adult rescue dogs with no history: run the full puppy system; without puppy bladders it usually clicks in days to a few weeks.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to potty train a puppy?

Most puppies are reliably trained in 4–8 weeks of consistent scheduling, though occasional adolescent accidents into month six are normal. Small breeds often take longer — tiny bladders, tiny margins.

### How often should I take my puppy out to pee?

Every hour at 8–10 weeks, stretching with age (months + 1 rule): roughly every 2 hours at 3 months, every 3 at 4 months — plus always after waking, eating, and playing.

### Should I punish my puppy for accidents?

No — punishment teaches puppies to hide when they go, which is the exact opposite of what you want. Interrupt calmly if caught mid-act; if found later, just clean with enzymatic cleaner and tighten supervision.

### Why is my potty-trained dog suddenly having accidents?

Rule out medical first — urinary tract infections are the classic cause of sudden regression and need a [vet](/dogs/health/signs-your-dog-needs-a-vet/). After that: schedule drift, too much freedom too fast, stress, or adolescence.
