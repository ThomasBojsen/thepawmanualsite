# Dog Dental Care: Brushing, Proven Chews, and Vet Cleanings

**Good dog dental care comes down to three things: brushing at home (daily is best), choosing chews and other products that have proven they reduce plaque or tartar, and a vet check of the mouth at least once a year, with cleanings under anesthesia when needed.** Plaque starts hardening into tartar within about a day, and the gum disease it causes is the main reason dogs lose teeth. Caught early, gum inflammation can be reversed. Left alone, it becomes periodontitis, which destroys the support around the teeth for good. Here's how to keep your dog's mouth healthy, which products are worth the money, and what to expect from a professional cleaning.

## Why dog dental care matters

Plaque is a sticky film of bacteria that builds up on teeth every day. Cornell's vet school notes that minerals in saliva can start hardening it into tartar (calculus) within as little as 24 hours, and tartar can't be brushed off.

The Merck Veterinary Manual describes how it progresses:

- **Gingivitis:** red or purple gums that bleed easily. Merck says it's often first noticed at about 2 years of age, and it can be reversed with proper cleaning.
- **Periodontitis:** the deeper stage, where the tissue and bone holding the teeth are destroyed. Merck says it usually begins at 4 to 6 years of age and is irreversible.

Small breeds usually have more trouble with periodontitis than large breeds, according to Merck, so they often need closer attention. Dental problems aren't only about the mouth either: the AVMA says dental problems can cause, or be caused by, other health problems, and Cornell notes that mouth bacteria entering the bloodstream can affect organs such as the kidneys, liver, and heart.

## Signs your dog needs dental care

Dogs are good at hiding mouth pain, so watch for small changes. The AVMA lists these as reasons to have your dog's teeth checked:

- Bad breath
- Broken or loose teeth
- Extra teeth or retained baby teeth
- Teeth that are discolored or covered in tartar
- Abnormal chewing, drooling, or dropping food from the mouth
- Reduced appetite or refusal to eat
- Pain in or around the mouth
- Bleeding from the mouth
- Swelling around the mouth

Some dogs simply become irritable or head-shy. Be careful when you look into a sore mouth, as a dog in pain may bite. For a wider checklist, see our [dog symptoms guide](/dogs/health/symptoms/).

## How to brush your dog's teeth

Brushing is the most effective thing you can do at home: Cornell calls it the most effective home care for preventing dental disease, and the Veterinary Oral Health Council (VOHC) calls it the gold standard. Merck says daily brushing is best.

Build up slowly over one to two weeks:

1. **Start with the toothpaste.** Let your dog lick a little dog toothpaste off your finger, and reward them.
2. **Touch the teeth.** Lift the lip and rub your finger along the outside of a few teeth. Keep sessions short and positive.
3. **Add a finger brush or gauze.** Work along the gumline in small circles.
4. **Move to a soft dog toothbrush.** Angle it toward the gumline and brush the outer surfaces. Merck notes that only the outside surfaces need brushing, so you don't need to open the mouth.
5. **Make it routine.** Same time each day, followed by a reward or a walk.

**Use dog toothpaste only.** Merck says toothpastes made for people shouldn't be used on dogs, and the FDA lists toothpastes and mouthwashes among products that can contain xylitol, which is toxic to dogs.

### How often should you brush?

Daily is best. If your dog won't accept a brush yet, Merck suggests wiping the teeth with gauze at least every 2 to 3 days. Because plaque starts to harden within a day, longer gaps let tartar build up.

### When to start

As early as possible. Puppies who get used to having their mouths handled accept brushing far more easily as adults. Our [puppy teething guide](/dogs/puppy-care/puppy-teething-guide/) covers how to start gently while baby teeth are changing.

## Dental chews, diets, and water additives: look for the VOHC seal

Most dental products make claims, but few prove them. The VOHC seal is the simplest filter: it's given to products that have shown, in trials that meet the council's standards, that they slow plaque, tartar, or both. The VOHC doesn't test products itself; it reviews the company's results.

| Product | VOHC seal? | Good to know |
|---|---|---|
| Toothbrush and dog toothpaste | Yes | The most effective option when used daily |
| Dental chews and treats | Yes | A helpful add-on to brushing; pick the right size for your dog's weight and count the calories |
| Dental diets | Yes | Kibble designed to scrub the teeth; useful if brushing isn't possible |
| Water additives | Yes | Only some products have the seal; check the list |
| Oral gels and sprays | Yes | Can help dogs that won't tolerate a brush |
| Bones, antlers, hooves, hard nylon toys | No | Merck and Cornell both warn these can fracture teeth |

The seal can be for plaque, for tartar, or for both, so check which one a product has. VOHC keeps an up-to-date list of accepted products on its website. For safer chewing options, see our [chew toy guide](/dogs/products/best-dog-toys-for-chewers/), and count chews in your dog's [daily treat budget](/dogs/food/how-much-should-i-feed-my-dog/).

## How to clean your dog's teeth without brushing

If your dog won't accept a toothbrush yet, these are the next best options, roughly from most to least useful:

1. **Gauze or dental wipes** on your finger, at least every 2 to 3 days.
2. **VOHC-accepted chews**, given daily.
3. **A VOHC-accepted dental diet**, if your vet agrees it suits your dog.
4. **VOHC-accepted water additives or oral gels.**

None of these reach below the gumline, and none remove tartar that's already there. Keep working toward brushing, and keep the yearly checkups.

## Professional dental cleaning: what actually happens

The AVMA recommends a vet check of your dog's teeth and gums at least once a year. When a cleaning is needed, it's done under general anesthesia. According to Cornell, a full examination and dental X-rays are only possible when the dog is anesthetized. The cleaning covers the teeth above and below the gumline, where most dental disease occurs, and each tooth is probed for pockets.

X-rays matter more than most owners expect. The American Veterinary Dental College (AVDC) cites studies in which X-rays of dogs whose teeth showed no visible problems still turned up findings in roughly 28–42% of cases.

### How often do dogs need a cleaning?

It depends on the dog. Small breeds, older dogs, and dogs that aren't brushed usually need cleanings more often. The AKC Canine Health Foundation, citing the American Animal Hospital Association's dental guidelines, says yearly exams and cleanings should begin at about 1 year of age for small breeds and 2 years for large breeds. Your vet will set the schedule from what they see at each checkup.

### How much does a dog dental cleaning cost?

Prices vary widely by region and clinic, and by what the vet finds. A routine cleaning with X-rays costs much less than one that needs extractions or other treatment, so ask for a written estimate that covers both scenarios. Regular brushing is the most reliable way to keep future bills down. Our guide to [pet insurance](/pet-guides/pet-insurance/) explains what plans typically do and don't cover.

### What about anesthesia-free cleaning?

The AVDC calls cleaning teeth on an awake pet inappropriate and says it isn't an effective way to prevent dental disease. Scraping visible tartar can't reach below the gumline, where disease actually develops, and the AVDC warns it gives owners a false sense of accomplishment. The AVMA adds that it can also injure the pet or the person doing it.

### Is anesthesia safe for older dogs?

No anesthesia is 100% risk-free, and the AVDC says the decision should take into account each dog's general health. Talk through the risks and benefits with your vet, because leaving painful, infected teeth untreated has costs too. Ask your vet what health checks they do beforehand and how the dog is monitored during the procedure.

## Is it too late to save my dog's teeth?

Not necessarily. Merck says gingivitis can be reversed with proper cleaning. Periodontitis can't be reversed, but treatment can stop it getting worse and save the teeth that still have good support. Badly damaged teeth may need to be removed. That sounds drastic, but Merck notes that dogs do surprisingly well without the affected teeth, and removing a painful tooth often makes a dog more comfortable. After treatment, daily home care keeps the problem from coming back.

> **Veterinary disclaimer:** This guide is educational and can't diagnose your dog. Bad breath, broken teeth, bleeding gums, or trouble eating need a licensed veterinarian: dogs often hide mouth pain until it's severe.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I brush my dog's teeth?

Daily is best, according to the Merck Veterinary Manual. If your dog won't accept a toothbrush yet, wipe the teeth with gauze at least every 2 to 3 days while you build up to brushing. Plaque starts to harden into tartar within about a day.

### Do dental chews actually work?

Chews that carry the VOHC seal have shown they reduce plaque, tartar, or both, and they help as an addition to brushing. Chews without the seal haven't proven it. Very hard chews such as bones, antlers, and hooves can break teeth.

### Is anesthesia-free dog teeth cleaning safe?

The American Veterinary Dental College calls it inappropriate and not effective at preventing dental disease. It can't clean below the gumline, where disease develops, and it doesn't allow X-rays. Talk to your vet about anesthesia concerns instead.

### How much does a dog dental cleaning cost?

It varies widely by region, clinic, and what the vet finds. Extractions and extra treatment cost more than a routine cleaning with X-rays, so ask for a written estimate covering both. Daily brushing is the best way to keep future costs down.

### Can I use human toothpaste on my dog?

No. Toothpastes made for people shouldn't be used on dogs, and some contain xylitol, which the FDA warns is toxic to dogs. Use a toothpaste made for dogs, ideally one with the VOHC seal.
