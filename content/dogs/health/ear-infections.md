# Dog Ear Infections: Signs, Treatment, and Why They Keep Coming Back

**A dog ear infection usually shows up as head shaking, scratching at one ear, a bad smell, redness, and brown or yellow discharge.** Most are infections of the outer ear canal (otitis externa) caused by yeast, bacteria, or both, and they need a vet visit: the vet checks the eardrum and looks at a swab under the microscope before choosing drops, because the wrong product can make things worse. They're common. In a UK study of more than 22,000 dogs, 7.3% had an outer-ear infection diagnosed in a single year. And infections that keep coming back usually have a cause underneath, such as an [allergy](/dogs/health/allergies/), that has to be managed too.

## What are the signs of a dog ear infection?

The classic signs are head shaking and scratching or pawing at the ear, followed by odor, redness, and discharge. Watch for:

- Head shaking, or rubbing one side of the face on the floor or furniture
- Scratching or pawing at one or both ears
- A musty, sour, or yeasty smell from the ear
- Redness, swelling, or scaly skin inside the ear flap
- Discharge or more wax than usual: brown, yellow, black, or bloody
- Pain: flinching, yelping, or ducking away when you touch the head
- A swollen, puffy ear flap (an aural hematoma), which needs its own vet treatment

One ear or both can be affected. Don't try to diagnose the type of infection from the color of the gunk: according to the Merck Veterinary Manual, dark discharge often means yeast or mites, but it can also appear with bacterial or mixed infections. For a wider checklist of what's normal and what isn't, see our [dog symptoms guide](/dogs/health/symptoms/).

## When is a dog ear infection an emergency?

Most ear infections need a vet appointment within a day or two, not an emergency room. Balance problems, facial changes, or severe pain are different: they can mean the infection has reached the middle or inner ear, and that needs a vet the same day.

| What you see | How fast |
|---|---|
| Head tilt with stumbling, falling, or circling; eyes flicking side to side; vomiting along with balance loss | Same day |
| Drooping face or eyelid, trouble blinking, or one pupil smaller than the other | Same day |
| Sudden, severe pain; a dog that won't let you near the head; lethargy or refusing food | Same day |
| Swollen, puffy ear flap | Within a day or two |
| Head shaking, scratching, odor, redness, discharge | Within a day or two |

Merck notes that inner-ear infection is rare in dogs, and that infection spreading from the inner ear to the brain is a possible complication of it. That's why balance and facial signs move the visit up. Our [vet urgency guide](/dogs/health/signs-your-dog-needs-a-vet/) covers the other signs that can't wait.

## What causes ear infections in dogs?

Yeast and bacteria are usually the infection, not the cause. They live in healthy ears in small numbers and overgrow when something else changes the ear first. Vets sort those triggers into three groups:

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Starts the problem** | Food or environmental allergies, ear mites, a grass seed or other object in the canal, hormone diseases such as hypothyroidism, some autoimmune skin diseases |
| **Makes it easier** | Floppy, narrow, or hairy ear canals; moisture from swimming or baths; growths or polyps that block the canal |
| **Keeps it going** | A canal that has thickened or narrowed from long-term inflammation, infection in the middle ear, bacteria that resist common medicines |

This is why one bottle of drops rarely ends the story for a dog with repeat infections: the drops treat the overgrowth, not what started it.

### Which dog breeds get the most ear infections?

In the UK VetCompass study, the breeds with the highest odds of outer-ear infection compared with crossbreeds were:

| Breed | Odds vs. crossbreeds |
|---|---|
| Basset Hound | 5.87× |
| Chinese Shar Pei | 3.44× |
| Labradoodle | 2.95× |
| Beagle | 2.54× |
| [Golden Retriever](/dogs/breeds/golden-retriever/) | 2.23× |

Ear shape mattered too: dogs with hanging ears had 1.76 times the odds and dogs with V-shaped folded ears 1.84 times the odds of dogs with upright ears. Chihuahuas, Border Collies, Yorkshire Terriers, and Jack Russell Terriers had lower odds than crossbreeds. [Labrador Retrievers](/dogs/breeds/labrador-retriever/) made up the most cases (154 of 1,631), mainly because they were the most common breed in the study.

### Yeast vs. bacterial ear infection: can you tell the difference?

Not reliably at home. Yeast (usually *Malassezia*) and bacteria can look and smell similar, and many dogs have both. The vet tells them apart with cytology: a swab of the discharge, stained and checked under a microscope, which shows yeast, round bacteria such as staph, or rod-shaped bacteria such as *Pseudomonas*. That matters because yeast needs antifungal treatment and bacteria need antibacterial treatment, and rod-shaped bacteria often don't respond to the usual first-choice antibiotics.

### Ear mites or ear infection?

Ear mites cause intense itching and head shaking that look a lot like an infection, and they can lead to one. A vet confirms mites by spotting them, their eggs, or their droppings in the ear debris. Mites spread by direct contact, so Merck advises treating every animal that has been in contact with the affected pet, not just the one scratching.

## How do vets treat a dog ear infection?

Treatment usually means medicated ear drops chosen from what the swab shows, plus cleaning, a treatment course of a few weeks, and a recheck. The usual steps:

1. **Exam.** The vet looks down the canal with an otoscope to check for foreign objects, debris, mites, and a torn eardrum. A very painful ear may need sedation for this.
2. **Swab before cleaning.** Merck advises sampling the discharge before any cleaning. If you can, skip cleaning your dog's ears before the appointment unless your vet tells you otherwise.
3. **Cleaning.** Wax and discharge can block ear medicine, so the vet cleans the canal first.
4. **Medicated drops.** Most combine an antibacterial, an antifungal, and an anti-inflammatory. If the eardrum is torn, the vet avoids ingredients that can damage hearing, such as aminoglycoside antibiotics.
5. **Pills when needed.** Merck says antibiotics by mouth aren't required for a simple outer-ear infection but are used when the middle ear is involved, and severe cases may get them.
6. **Rechecks until the swab is clear.** Most new infections take 2–4 weeks. Long-standing ones can take months, and some dogs need lifelong management.

Finish the full course even when the ear looks better by day three. "Looks fine from outside" and "clear under the microscope" are different findings, and stopping early is one way an infection comes back. For ears that have scarred shut after years of disease, surgery that removes the ear canal is the last resort.

## Can you treat a dog ear infection at home without a vet?

Not safely, in most cases. Home treatment can't check the eardrum or tell yeast from bacteria, and several popular remedies irritate an inflamed ear. Here's how the common ones hold up:

| Remedy | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Vinegar or apple cider vinegar mixes | **Skip.** Merck warns that vinegar dilutions and other home remedies can worsen swelling and discharge |
| Hydrogen peroxide | **Skip.** Cornell's vet school advises avoiding hydrogen peroxide in the ear |
| Alcohol or alcohol-based cleaners | **Skip.** Same Cornell advice; it stings inflamed skin |
| Oils and other kitchen remedies | **Skip.** Even products that are fine in a healthy ear can irritate an inflamed one (Merck) |
| Leftover drops from a past infection | **Skip.** They may be the wrong type for this infection, and some can harm a torn eardrum |
| Human antibiotics or leftover pills | **Skip.** Outer-ear infections are mainly treated with drops, and the bacteria involved often don't respond to common antibiotics |
| Over-the-counter antihistamines | **Won't cure it.** They don't treat infection; ask your vet before giving any human medicine |
| Vet-recommended ear cleaner | **Not a cure.** Useful for upkeep, but stop and call the vet if cleaning hurts |

What you can do today: book a vet visit, stop the scratching from making the skin raw, keep water out of the ears, and note when the signs started. Don't put anything into an ear that hasn't been examined.

### What if you can't afford a vet?

Call your clinic and ask for an estimate for an exam and ear cytology only, and ask whether they offer payment plans. Low-cost clinics, animal shelters, humane societies, and veterinary school teaching hospitals often offer cheaper exams. Treating early is usually the cheaper route: a long-standing infection can mean months of treatment, rechecks, and sometimes surgery. Our guides to [pet costs](/pet-guides/pet-costs/) and [pet insurance](/pet-guides/pet-insurance/) help you plan for the next one.

## How long does a dog ear infection last?

With treatment, most new outer-ear infections clear in about 2–4 weeks, according to Merck. Long-standing infections can take months. Without treatment, an outer-ear infection can become chronic and spread inward: Merck notes that in dogs, middle-ear infection most often follows a long-standing outer-ear infection. Middle- and inner-ear infection can affect hearing and balance.

## Why does my dog keep getting ear infections?

A second infection within months is a pattern, not bad luck. Merck's summary is blunt: unless every cause is treated, the condition often returns. Work through the likely causes with your vet:

- **Allergy:** a food trial with a [diet for allergic dogs](/dogs/food/dog-food-for-allergies/) or management of environmental allergies. Itchy paws, belly, or face alongside the ears point this way ([why dogs itch](/dogs/health/why-is-my-dog-itching/)).
- **Hormones:** blood tests for conditions such as hypothyroidism.
- **The canal itself:** narrowing, polyps, or a hidden foreign object.
- **Resistant bacteria:** a culture to pick a medicine that works.
- **The middle ear:** imaging if infections keep returning.

Then finish each course and go back for the recheck. Buying a bigger bottle of cleaner doesn't fix any of these.

## How to clean your dog's ears safely

Clean healthy ears only when you can see dirt or debris: Cornell notes that over-cleaning can itself irritate the ear. During treatment your vet may want daily cleaning at first, then every 1–2 weeks. The method, based on Merck and Cornell:

1. Use a cleaner your vet recommends, not alcohol or hydrogen peroxide.
2. Fill the ear canal with the cleaner.
3. Massage the base of the ear for about 20–30 seconds, until you hear it squish.
4. Let your dog shake out the loosened debris.
5. Wipe the ear flap and the opening of the canal with a cotton ball or gauze. Never use cotton swabs: they push debris deeper.
6. If your dog is getting ear medicine, wait a few minutes after cleaning before putting it in.

If your dog seems to be in pain, stop and call your vet. Dry your dog's ears after swimming and baths, and check the ear flaps as part of your weekly [grooming routine](/dogs/grooming/).

> **Veterinary disclaimer:** This page is education, not diagnosis. Ear infections need a veterinary exam and prescription treatment. Home care supports that treatment; it doesn't replace it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a dog ear infection go away on its own?

Don't count on it. There's no reliable way to tell at home whether an infection will clear, and waiting risks a longer infection that can spread to the middle ear. A vet exam and a swab tell you what you're dealing with.

### Is a dog ear infection contagious to people or other pets?

A yeast or bacterial ear infection usually comes from your dog's own ear environment rather than being caught from another animal. Ear mites are the exception: they spread by direct contact, so every pet in contact with the infested one needs treatment. Wash your hands after cleaning or medicating the ears.

### What does a dog ear infection smell like?

Usually musty, sour, or yeasty, and sometimes foul. Smell alone can't tell yeast from bacteria: a swab can. A new ear odor plus head shaking is a reason to book a vet visit.

### Can I put hydrogen peroxide or vinegar in my dog's ear?

No. Cornell's vet school advises avoiding hydrogen peroxide and alcohol in the ear, and the Merck Veterinary Manual warns that vinegar mixtures can worsen swelling and discharge. Use a cleaner your vet recommends.

### Can a dog ear infection spread to the brain?

Rarely. An outer-ear infection can spread to the middle and inner ear, and Merck lists meningitis or a brain abscess as possible complications of inner-ear infection, which is itself rare in dogs. A head tilt with loss of balance, circling, or a drooping face needs a vet the same day.
