# Cat Food Calculator: How Many Calories Does Your Cat Need?

**A 10 lb (4.5 kg) indoor, neutered adult cat needs roughly 180–250 calories a day, and a more active neutered cat of the same size about 260.** That's usually less than the feeding guide on the bag suggests. Enter your cat's weight, life stage and lifestyle below, add the calories from your food's label, and the calculator turns it into cans, cups or grams per day and per meal.

## How many calories does a cat need per day?

Many indoor, neutered adult cats need about 1.0 to 1.2 times their resting energy requirement: roughly 150–200 kcal a day at 8 lb, 180–250 kcal at 10 lb and 230–320 kcal for a large 12–14 lb cat. The calculator uses 1.0 for an indoor, mostly calm cat and 1.2 for a playful cat or one with outdoor access.

| Cat | Daily calories (approx.) |
|---|---|
| 8 lb (3.6 kg) indoor adult, neutered | 150–200 kcal |
| 10 lb (4.5 kg) indoor adult, neutered | 180–250 kcal |
| 12–14 lb (5.4–6.4 kg) large-frame adult | 230–320 kcal |
| Kitten | about 2.5 times resting energy, recalculated as it grows |

Treat these as starting points. The real test is the rib check two to three weeks later: ribs easy to feel under a thin cover, a visible waist from above. Our guide to [how much to feed your cat](/cats/food/how-much-should-i-feed-my-cat/) covers body condition and meal feeding in detail.

## How this calculator works

The calculator uses the standard veterinary energy formula: it works out your cat's resting energy requirement (RER), then multiplies it by a factor for life stage and lifestyle.

**Step 1, resting energy:** RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)^0.75.

**Step 2, daily energy:** RER × a factor from this table.

| Your cat | Factor used |
|---|---|
| Kitten under 12 months | 2.5 × RER |
| Neutered adult, indoor and calm | 1.0 × RER |
| Neutered adult, playful or outdoor access | 1.2 × RER |
| Intact adult | 1.2–1.4 × RER |
| Neutered senior (11 years and up) | 1.1–1.2 × RER |

Senior cats are never set below the adult number: many cats over about 10 to 12 need more calories, not fewer, because digestion becomes less efficient with age.

**Step 3, portions:** daily calories divided by your food's calorie density. US labels give kcal per can or per cup; European labels usually give energy per 100 g or per kg. The calculator accepts either.

## How do I convert calories into cans, cups or grams?

Divide the daily calories by the calories in one can, one cup or 100 g of your food. A cat needing 220 kcal, eating a wet food with 90 kcal per can, needs about 2.4 cans a day; on a dry food with 400 kcal per cup, about half a cup.

Wet foods vary from about 70 to 200 kcal per can and dry foods from about 300 to more than 500 kcal per cup, so the label always beats a rule of thumb. If you feed both, split the calories between them, for example half from each, rather than adding the two amounts together. If your label gives energy only in kilojoules (kJ), divide by 4.184 to get kilocalories (kcal). Treats belong inside a 10% ceiling, taken out of the meal rather than added on top.

## When should you not rely on a calculator?

Ask your vet instead of a calculator when your cat needs to lose weight, is losing weight without trying, is a kitten that has stopped gaining, or has a medical condition. Cats must never be crash-dieted: going without enough food can trigger hepatic lipidosis, a serious liver disease, so safe feline weight loss is slow and vet-supervised.

In cats, unexplained weight loss is often the bigger warning sign than gain, especially in older cats. See [signs your cat needs a vet](/cats/health/signs-your-cat-needs-a-vet/) for what's urgent, and our [kitten food](/cats/food/kitten-food/) and [senior cat food](/cats/food/senior-cat-food/) guides for life-stage feeding.

> **Veterinary disclaimer:** This calculator gives a general estimate for healthy cats, not a feeding prescription. Weight-loss programs, underweight cats, kittens falling behind, and cats with medical conditions need portions set with a licensed veterinarian.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much should I feed my cat per day?

A typical 10 lb indoor, neutered adult cat needs roughly 180–250 kcal a day. Divide your cat's number by the calories per can, cup or 100 g on your food's label to get the portion, then adjust by about 10% if your cat gains or loses weight.

### How many cans of wet food should a cat eat a day?

It depends entirely on the can: wet foods range from about 70 to 200 kcal per can. A cat needing 220 kcal eats about 2.4 cans of a 90 kcal food, but just over one can of a 200 kcal food, so always check the label.

### Do indoor cats need fewer calories?

Usually, yes. Many indoor, neutered cats need about 1.0 times their resting energy requirement, compared with the standard 1.2 for a neutered adult, because they move less. That's one reason so many indoor cats become overweight on bag-guide portions.

### Can I put my overweight cat on a diet myself?

Not a fast one. Cutting a cat's food sharply can trigger hepatic lipidosis, a serious liver disease, so weight loss in cats should be slow and planned with your vet.

  - Factors: Merck Veterinary Manual table (https://www.merckvetmanual.com/multimedia/table/daily-maintenance-energy-requirements-for-dogs-and-cats): neutered 1.2, intact 1.4, inactive/obese-prone 1.0, kitten 2.5. Pet Nutrition Alliance (https://petnutritionalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/MER.RER_.PNA_.pdf): kitten 2–3.
  - "Indoor neutered about 1.0": FEDIAF 2025 puts neutered and/or indoor cats at 52–75 kcal/kg^0.67, about 0.66–0.95 × RER at 4.5 kg (https://europeanpetfood.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/FEDIAF-Nutritional-Guidelines_2025-ONLINE.pdf); AAHA 2021 notes sedentary pets may need less than its factors; Bermingham et al. found NRC estimates overshoot cats' needs (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4196927/). Caveat: WSAVA's adult-cat chart gives 240–270 kcal for a 4.5 kg cat (about 1.1–1.25 × RER), above the article's 180–250 band, which is why the page says "about 1.0 to 1.2" and the calculator offers both.
  - Seniors: no guideline gives a number. AAHA 2023 Senior Care Guidelines: energy needs fall to about age 10 and then rise, digestion weakens in many cats over 11 (https://www.aaha.org/wp-content/uploads/globalassets/02-guidelines/2023-aaha-senior-care-guidelines-for-dogs-and-cats/resources/2023-aaha-senior-care-guidelines-for-dogs-and-cats.pdf). Calculator never goes below the adult factor.
  - Kitten age bands (3.0 under 4 months etc.) appear in no source directly, so the calculator uses the flat, sourced 2.5 and a note that young kittens can need up to 3 (PNA range).
  - 10% treat ceiling: AAHA 2021. Wet-can and dry-cup ranges repeat the feeding article; the only peer-reviewed survey found covers weight-loss diets only (canned cat 78–172 kcal/can, dry 235–480 kcal/cup), so no extra number is cited.
  - Unverified Tufts figures that circulate online ("8–10 lb cat 200–250 kcal") were deliberately not used.
