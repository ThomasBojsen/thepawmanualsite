# Best Litter Boxes: The Rules Beat the Features

Litter box success is three rules, not one product: **size** (1.5× your cat's length: most store boxes fail this for anything bigger than a small cat), **count** (one per cat *plus one*, in separate locations), and **openness** (most cats prefer uncovered: lids are a human feature). Get the rules right and a $12 pan outperforms a $600 robot with the rules wrong. Here's the honest tour of the box types, the setup math, and when the premium options actually earn their price. *Links may earn us a commission: [affiliate disclosure](/affiliate-disclosure/); it never changes a recommendation.*

## The three rules, explained

Litter box success comes down to three rules: size at 1.5× the cat's length, one box per cat plus one in separate locations, and an uncovered box by default.

1. **1.5× the cat, nose to tail-base.** A cat should turn, dig, and posture without touching walls: cramped boxes get perch-and-dangle usage, then avoidance. Big cats ([Maine Coon owners, hello](/cats/breeds/maine-coon/)) often do best with a storage tote conversion: cheap, huge, high-sided.
2. **n+1, separated.** Two cats = three boxes *in different territory zones*: three boxes in one laundry room is one box with spares. Multi-cat friction over boxes is a leading [avoidance driver](/cats/behavior/); separation is the fix. One per floor in multi-story homes.
3. **Uncovered by default.** Covers trap odor *inside at cat nose level*, block escape routes (an ambush risk in multi-cat homes), and hide the [health data your scoop provides](/cats/health/). Covered works for some easygoing cats, but when problems appear, the lid is the first thing to remove.

## Box types, honestly

An open, uncovered pan is the default winner for most cats, stainless steel is worth the upgrade since it won't absorb odor the way plastic does, and automatic or robotic boxes solve scooping-avoidance, not cat problems.

| Type | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Open pan (large)** | The default winner: cheap, accepted, monitorable |
| High-sided open | Best of both for litter-kickers and vertical pee-ers; keep one low entry point for [seniors](/cats/food/senior-cat-food/) and [kittens](/cats/kitten-care/how-to-litter-train-a-kitten/) |
| Stainless steel pans | Genuinely good upgrade: won't absorb odor into scratches like aging plastic; replace plastic boxes yearly, steel lasts |
| Covered/hooded | Human preference; fine for tolerant cats, first suspect at avoidance |
| Top-entry | Dog-proof and low-tracking: for young, agile cats only; unfair to arthritic ones |
| **Automatic/robot** | Solves *scooping-avoidance*, not cat problems: some cats fear the mechanism, you lose daily [output monitoring](/cats/health/signs-your-cat-needs-a-vet/), multi-cat data merges, and entry sizes exclude big cats. For an already-happy cat and a scooping-averse human: legitimate. As a fix for box problems: usually backwards |

Liners: mostly annoy (claws snag, urine pools in folds). Litter mats: yes ([tracking answer](/cats/products/best-cat-litter/)).

## Placement: think like prey that needs a bathroom

Quiet but not isolated, sightlines and escape routes (corners with two exits beat closets with one), **away from food and water** (cats refuse to dine at the toilet), away from appliances that bang or spin ([the washing-machine aversion](/cats/kitten-care/how-to-litter-train-a-kitten/)), and never behind a door that swings shut. Senior addendum: arthritis relocates cats to whatever box requires no stairs and no climbing: a ground-floor low-entry box is cheap [pain accommodation](/cats/health/symptoms/).

**The standing rule, one more time:** a cat avoiding its box gets a [medical workup first](/cats/health/signs-your-cat-needs-a-vet/): urinary pain teaches box-fear fast, and a [straining male cat is an emergency](/cats/health/signs-your-cat-needs-a-vet/). Setup fixes come second.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many litter boxes do I need?

One per cat plus one, in *separate* locations: two cats need three boxes in different zones. Boxes clustered in one room count as one box with spares.

### Are covered litter boxes bad?

Not universally, but they're a human preference: covers trap odor at cat level, block escape routes, and hide health information. Tolerant cats manage; at the first sign of avoidance, the lid goes.

### Are automatic litter boxes worth it?

For an already-content cat and a scooping-averse human, they're a legitimate convenience. They don't fix cat problems: some cats fear them, big cats don't fit, and you lose the daily output monitoring that catches illness early.

### Why is my cat peeing right next to the box?

Medical first: urinary discomfort creates box-association pain, and a straining male is an emergency. Then setup: box too small, too dirty, wrong litter, bad location, or multi-cat friction. Next-to-the-box is usually a cat *trying* to comply.
