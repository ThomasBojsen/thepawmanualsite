# Kitten Vaccination Schedule: Which Shots, When, and Why the Series Works This Way

Kittens receive their core vaccine, **FVRCP**, as a **series every 3–4 weeks from about 6–8 weeks of age until at least 16 weeks**, plus rabies per state law, and (per the 2020 AAHA/AAFP Feline Vaccination Guidelines) **FeLV vaccination for all kittens.** The multi-dose logic mirrors [the puppy version](/dogs/puppy-care/puppy-vaccination-schedule/): maternal antibodies from mother's milk neutralize vaccines on an unpredictable, fading timeline, and the series exists to close that gap, which is why the final dose at 16+ weeks is the one that must not be missed. Indoor-cat plans included; that question gets its own honest section below.

## The schedule at a glance

| Age | Typical vaccines |
|---|---|
| 6–8 weeks | FVRCP #1: often given shelter/breeder-side; check the paperwork |
| 10–12 weeks | FVRCP #2 · FeLV #1 |
| 14–16 weeks | FVRCP #3 (**the ≥16-week dose is the critical one**) · FeLV #2 · rabies (state law typically 12–16 weeks) |
| 12–16 months | FVRCP booster · rabies booster · FeLV per risk |
| Adult | Rabies per state law (1–3 yr) · FVRCP typically every 3 years · FeLV annually for outdoor/exposure-risk cats |

Your vet tailors timing and products to region, law, and your kitten's history. This table is the map, not the prescription. AAHA/AAFP guidance now recommends considering the FVRCP booster at 6 months rather than 12, to close a window of vaccine susceptibility. Ask your vet whether that earlier timing fits your kitten.

## What FVRCP actually covers

- **P (Panleukopenia):** feline parvovirus: hardy in the environment, devastating and frequently fatal in unvaccinated kittens. This letter alone justifies the series; it's the feline parallel to [parvo in puppies](/dogs/puppy-care/puppy-vaccination-schedule/).
- **FVR (Feline viral rhinotracheitis, herpesvirus)** and **C (Calicivirus):** the two engines of [feline URIs](/cats/health/why-is-my-cat-sneezing/). Vaccination doesn't fully block infection but sharply reduces severity, and since herpes goes latent for life and flares under stress, arriving at that lifelong arrangement *vaccinated* matters.
- **FeLV (feline leukemia virus):** spread cat-to-cat via saliva and close contact; a leading infectious disease of young cats, and the most common cause of cancer in cats. Per the 2020 AAHA/AAFP Feline Vaccination Guidelines, the two-dose series is recommended for **all kittens** (young immune systems are most vulnerable, and future lifestyles have a way of changing), with adult boosters continued for at-risk (outdoor/multi-cat-exposure) cats.
- **Rabies:** legally required in most U.S. states, fatal, zoonotic: the one the law, not just the vet, mandates.

## "But my cat will be indoors": the honest section

Indoor-only still means core vaccines: **panleukopenia travels on shoes and hands** (it survives long in the environment), **rabies is law** and bats do enter homes, escape happens ([ask the GPS-tracker demographic](/dogs/products/best-gps-dog-trackers/)), and future boarding, vet stays, or a second cat all change exposure math. Where indoor status legitimately matters is the *adult* schedule: FeLV boosters become risk-based, and your vet will right-size the plan. Skipping the kitten series on an indoor plan is the one version with no defensible math.

## Side effects, costs, and one feline-specific note

**Normal for a day:** sleepiness, mild soreness, small appetite dip. **Vet immediately:** facial swelling, hives, vomiting, breathing trouble: rare acute reactions, typically within minutes to hours ([emergency guide](/cats/health/signs-your-cat-needs-a-vet/)). **The feline-specific note:** injection-site sarcomas are a rare but real feline phenomenon: modern practice manages the risk with vaccine choices and standardized injection sites, and the disease risks being prevented far outweigh it; it's a fair question to ask your vet, not a reason to skip protection. **Costs:** commonly $75–$200 per visit depending on region and bundling, with low-cost clinics widely available; [insurance or wellness plans](/pet-guides/pet-insurance/) often cover routine vaccines, and the [cost guide](/pet-guides/pet-costs/) has the full first-year picture.

> **Veterinary disclaimer:** This page summarizes AAFP-style guidance for education. Your veterinarian sets your kitten's actual schedule: state law, regional risk, and your kitten's history all change the plan. Unknown vaccine history? Vets restart series safely all the time; don't guess.

## Frequently asked questions

### What shots do kittens need?

The FVRCP core series (every 3–4 weeks from 6–8 weeks until at least 16 weeks), rabies per state law, and, per the 2020 AAHA/AAFP Feline Vaccination Guidelines, the two-dose FeLV series for all kittens. Adult boosters follow at 12–16 months, then per protocol.

### Do indoor kittens really need vaccines?

Yes: panleukopenia arrives on shoes, rabies is legally required and bats enter homes, and escapes and lifestyle changes happen. Indoor status adjusts the *adult* booster plan (FeLV becomes risk-based), not the kitten series.

### When can my kitten meet other cats or go outside?

Socialization with known, vaccinated, healthy cats can proceed during the series; unknown-cat exposure (outdoors, shelters' general population) waits until 1–2 weeks after the final 16-week dose. The [8-week care guide](/cats/kitten-care/8-week-old-kitten-care/) covers the parallel handling-and-habituation work.

### What if my kitten misses a vaccine dose?

Call your vet: depending on the gap the series continues or restarts, and both are routine. The principle that survives any schedule shuffle: the final FVRCP dose lands at 16 weeks or later.
