# Kitten Care: The First Months, Planned Out

Kittens typically come home at 8–12 weeks (12+ is actually better — those extra litter weeks teach bite inhibition and cat social skills), and the early window is even more front-loaded than in puppies: the prime feline socialization period runs **2–7 weeks** — largely before you meet your kitten — which makes weeks 8–14 the catch-up-and-consolidate phase, and makes *where you get your kitten* half the temperament outcome. These guides cover the rest: setup, feeding, vaccines, and litter training (easier than you fear — it's mostly instinct plus logistics).

## Before pickup day

**[The new kitten checklist](/cats/kitten-care/new-kitten-checklist/)** covers the shopping and setup: litter box and litter ([choosing both](/cats/products/best-litter-boxes/)), food, bowls, [carrier](/cats/products/best-cat-carriers/) (non-negotiable from day one — the loose-cat-in-car plan fails at the first vet visit), scratching post, kitten-proofing (**string, thread, and hair ties are surgical emergencies** — [why](/cats/health/), lilies are lethal, cords get chewed), and the vet appointment booked for week one.

## The first days and weeks

**[8-week-old kitten care](/cats/kitten-care/8-week-old-kitten-care/)** walks the daily rhythm: the one-room start (a whole house overwhelms a kitten — territory grows in stages), sleep (kittens sleep 18–20 hours, [same as puppies](/dogs/puppy-care/8-week-old-puppy-schedule/)), play in short bursts (hands are never toys — the rule that prevents adult [play-biting](/cats/behavior/why-does-my-cat-bite-me/)), and gentle handling practice that pays for a decade of [vet visits](/cats/health/signs-your-cat-needs-a-vet/) and nail trims.

## Feeding and health

- **[Kitten feeding schedule](/cats/kitten-care/kitten-feeding-schedule/)** — meal counts by age, kitten-formula requirements ([why growth food matters](/cats/food/kitten-food/)), and the no-cow's-milk rule ([the myth](/cats/food/))
- **[Kitten vaccination schedule](/cats/kitten-care/kitten-vaccination-schedule/)** — the FVRCP core series every 3–4 weeks until 16+ weeks, rabies per law, FeLV for kittens per AAFP guidance, and the same maternal-antibody logic as [the puppy version](/dogs/puppy-care/puppy-vaccination-schedule/)

Spay/neuter typically lands around 5–6 months (before the first heat changes the math) — plan it at the vaccine visits.

## Litter training

**[How to litter train a kitten](/cats/kitten-care/how-to-litter-train-a-kitten/)** — the honest headline: most kittens arrive 90% trained by instinct and their mother. Your 10% is logistics: the right box (low entry, kitten-sized, in a quiet reachable spot), unscented fine litter, showing them the box after meals and naps, and *never* punishing accidents ([the same rule as dogs](/dogs/training/potty-training/), for the same reason). Accidents in a previously reliable kitten are a [health or setup flag](/cats/behavior/), not defiance.

## Frequently asked questions

### How old should a kitten be when you bring it home?

Minimum 8 weeks (many states regulate this), but 12–14 weeks is better — the extra litter time builds bite inhibition, confidence, and cat social skills you can't teach later. A too-early kitten is a harder kitten.

### What do I need for a new kitten?

Litter box and litter, kitten food, bowls, a carrier, a scratching post, toys, and a safe starter room — plus a vet appointment in week one. The [full checklist](/cats/kitten-care/new-kitten-checklist/) has the reasoning and the skip-list.

### When can kittens leave their mother?

Physically at 8 weeks; behaviorally, 12+ weeks is the better standard — weaning completes around 8 weeks, but social learning from mother and littermates continues well past it. Ethical breeders and rescues hold to this.

### Are kittens hard to litter train?

No — instinct does most of it. Provide a low-entry box in a quiet spot with unscented litter, place the kitten there after meals and naps, and never punish accidents. Persistent accidents are a [setup or health question](/cats/behavior/), not a training failure.
