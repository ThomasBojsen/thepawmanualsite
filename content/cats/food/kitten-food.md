# Kitten Food: What to Feed, How Often, and When to Switch

Kittens need a food carrying an AAFCO statement for **growth** (or "all life stages") until they're about **12 months old**: adult maintenance food shortchanges the protein, fat, calcium, and DHA that kitten development burns through. Growth is expensive: per pound, a kitten needs roughly two to three times an adult cat's calories, eaten in small, frequent meals through a stomach the size of a walnut. Get the formula and the frequency right, and kitten nutrition mostly runs itself.

## Why do kittens need growth formula food?

Kitten food is denser in everything that builds a cat: protein (obligate carnivore from day one, [the species rules](/cats/food/)), fat and calories, calcium/phosphorus in growth ratios, and DHA for brain and eye development. The label check is the same [60-second read](/cats/food/best-cat-food/) as adult food: AAFCO growth statement, named animal proteins, feeding-trial manufacturers preferred. One exception to the 12-month rule: slow-maturing giants like the [Maine Coon](/cats/breeds/maine-coon/) can justifiably stay on growth formula longer. Ask your vet at the [vaccine visits](/cats/kitten-care/kitten-vaccination-schedule/).

## Wet, dry, or both for kittens

Both work if the formula says growth; the case for **wet-forward feeding starts here**: it builds [hydration habits](/cats/food/wet-cat-food/) before the famously weak feline thirst drive matters, it's easier on baby teeth, and format preferences imprint young: a kitten raised on variety (wet and dry, different textures) becomes an adult who accepts diet changes, which future-you will thank you for the day a [therapeutic diet](/cats/health/) is prescribed. Kibble, if fed, should be kitten-sized; softening with warm water helps the youngest ([weaning context](/cats/kitten-care/kitten-feeding-schedule/)).

## Feeding schedule by age

Feeding frequency drops as kittens grow: four or more small meals a day until about 12 weeks, tapering to two or three meals by the first birthday.

| Age | Meals per day | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Weaning–12 weeks | 4+ (or measured free access) | Tiny stomach, huge needs; this is the one life stage where constant access is defensible |
| 3–6 months | 3–4 | Growth peak; [full schedule here](/cats/kitten-care/kitten-feeding-schedule/) |
| 6–12 months | 2–3 | Transitioning toward adult rhythm |
| ~12 months | Switch to adult food | Over 7–10 days, [the slow way](/cats/food/best-cat-food/) |

**No cow's milk at any stage**: most cats are lactose intolerant, and the saucer myth ends in diarrhea ([the myth-bust](/cats/food/)). Water plus proper food covers everything; orphaned kittens under weaning age need kitten milk replacer, never dairy.

## The switch to adult food

Around the first birthday, transition over 7–10 days to a quality [adult food](/cats/food/best-cat-food/), and recalculate portions immediately: keeping a neutered one-year-old on kitten calories is how the [61% overweight statistic](/cats/food/how-much-should-i-feed-my-cat/) recruits its members. Spay/neuter (typically ~5–6 months) drops energy needs before the food switch does: watch body condition through that gap.

> **Veterinary disclaimer:** This guide is general nutrition education. Kittens who won't eat, have diarrhea, or fall behind on growth need a veterinarian promptly: small bodies, small margins.

## Frequently asked questions

### When should kittens switch to adult cat food?

Around 12 months, transitioned over 7–10 days: earlier shortchanges growth nutrition. Slow-maturing large breeds like Maine Coons may stay on growth formula longer with vet guidance.

### How often should a kitten eat?

Four or more small meals (or measured free access) until about 12 weeks, then 3–4 meals to six months, tapering to the adult two-meal rhythm around the first birthday.

### Is wet or dry food better for kittens?

Either works if it's a growth formula, but a wet-forward mix builds hydration habits early and raises a flexible eater. Exposing kittens to both formats pays off for life.

### Can kittens drink milk?

No cow's milk: most cats are lactose intolerant and it causes diarrhea. Nursing kittens drink their mother's milk; orphans need commercial kitten milk replacer.
